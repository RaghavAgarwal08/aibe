    Workshops.

    Ninety minutes, laptops open, one skill. Written so that a student with no CS background and a student with no business background both leave able to use the thing.

    This is the running list of sessions on our docket. The order and the exact slate change each semester based on what members ask for — and if you want to lead one, you can.

    Discovery

    Mining Reddit, reviews and forums for real customer pain

    Where the complaints already are. Pull them, cluster them with a model, and find the problem worth solving before you write a line of anything.

    Discovery

    Using AI for market research and customer discovery

    Who is this actually for? Sizing a market, writing interview scripts, and getting to a real conversation with a stranger this week.

    Discovery

    Stress-testing your idea: AI as the sceptical investor

    Hand your idea to a model told to tear it apart, then answer it. The cheapest due diligence you will ever run.

    Build

    Creating MVPs and finding solutions to problems

    From a described problem to something clickable. The smallest version that lets a real person tell you you're wrong.

    Build

    Coding with Claude Code, OpenAI and friends

    For people who code and people who don't. How to drive these tools so they produce something you can actually maintain.

    Build

    Launching a micro-SaaS, solo

    The whole stack for a one-person product: auth, payments, hosting, support. What you can ignore and what you can't.

    Build

    Connecting AI to your tools with MCP

    Wiring models into the systems you already use, so they do work instead of describing it.

    Build

    Workflow automation

    Finding the repetitive thing you do every week and deleting it. Emails, scheduling, reporting, intake.

    Go to market

    AI marketing

    Positioning, copy, creative and channel testing — and where a model helps versus where it produces noise.

    Go to market

    AI for SEO: keyword research and posts that rank

    How search works now, what still earns traffic, and the difference between useful content and slop.

    Go to market

    Creating and selling digital products

    Templates, courses, ebooks. The fastest honest path from skill to revenue for a student.

    Run it

    Pricing strategy: tiers, anchors and experiments

    What to charge, how to find out, and why your first price is almost always too low.

    Run it

    Unit economics: are you actually making money?

    The arithmetic founders avoid. Cost per customer, cost per user, and the point where the numbers stop working.

    Run it

    Analytics and data insight with AI

    Getting from a pile of events to a decision. What to measure and what to ignore.

    Run it

    Running A/B tests as a one-person team

    Small-sample reality, what you can and can't conclude, and how to avoid fooling yourself.

    Run it

    Synthesising a hundred messages into the three things worth doing next.

    Documenting your own work well enough to hand it off. The skill that makes delegation possible.

    All semester

    Project teams.

    The reason the workshops exist. Every member is encouraged to be on a team, and being on one makes you an Active Member outright.

    The Project Slate

    At the start of each semester the Executive Board publishes a slate: a set of defined projects and challenge prompts, each with a goal, deliverables, milestones and a demo-day date. It exists so that nobody has to arrive with an idea already formed.

    Three ways onto a team

    • Pick one off the Slate. Find a project that sounds like your kind of problem and join it.
    • Get matched. Tell us roughly what you're interested in and we'll put you somewhere sensible.
    • Bring your own. Pitch your idea, recruit teammates, get it approved, and it becomes a team like any other.

    How a team works

    At least two people. A named team lead. Registered with the Secretary. Progress reported at milestones, and a presentation of what you built at demo day. The Director of Projects — or the Vice President, until that role is filled — handles team formation and keeps milestones honest.

    Not just software

    Services, media, physical products, marketplaces, content businesses. If it can be built and put in front of a customer, it can be a project.

    You own what you make

    Ideas, work product and any venture that grows out of a team belong to the people who created them. AIBE takes no ownership, equity or revenue share, and will not represent or imply University endorsement of anything you build. We do strongly encourage every team that plans to keep going to put a written founder agreement in place early — roles, contributions, ownership, decisions — before it gets complicated.

    Twice a semester

    Hackathons.

    A full day. Food, API credits and GPU compute provided. You use everything the workshops taught you to get from a blank page to an MVP and a business model, and pitch it that evening.

    The winning team gets funding to keep building and mentorship to go with it. It is also, in practice, where most teams find each other.

    Prize amount and sponsors for the first hackathon are still being confirmed — watch Discord.

    One full day

    Breakfast to evening pitches. Long enough to build something, short enough that you can't overthink it.

    Credits provided

    API credits and compute so that cost is never the reason a team stops.

    MVP and model

    Judging looks at the business as hard as the build. A beautiful demo with no customer doesn't win.

    Growing outward

    We intend to scale these up and eventually invite other schools, including alongside HackIllinois.

    The rest of it

    Everything else we run.

    Demo day

    End of each semester. Every project team shows what it built to judges, mentors and members. The closest thing we have to a final exam, and far more useful.

    Socials, monthly

    A kickoff social, co-founder “interviews” where you pitch yourself to potential teammates, and startup showcases. Teams form over food more reliably than over forms.

    Field trips

    Once or twice a year, out to the offices of AI companies. Members who want to go chip in roughly $40–50 toward transport.

    The weekly newsletter

    A member editorial team picks the best articles, papers and launches of the week and sends them out. A good job if you want to read widely and have a reason to.

    Idea sprints

    Short, sharp sessions where the whole room generates and tears apart ideas together. Where a lot of project teams start.

    Mentors

    Faculty, staff, alumni, graduate students and industry professionals who advise project teams and lead sessions. If you'd like to mentor, get in touch.

    The calendar for this semester lives on Discord.

    Dates, rooms, what's on this week, and who's speaking. It's the fastest way to find out what's actually happening.
